EU Cosmetics GMP Requirements: What Equipment Should Manufacturers Prioritize?

A practical guide to translating EU cosmetics GMP expectations into equipment-selection questions for controlled, repeatable manufacturing.

Published November 17, 2026 · HONEMIX Engineering Team
Executive perspective: EU cosmetics GMP is a manufacturing-system requirement, not a machine specification. Equipment investment should support controlled production, consistent processing, effective cleaning, traceable batches and reliable transfer and filling. A machine purchase alone does not establish GMP compliance.

1. What EU Cosmetics GMP Requires

For manufacturers placing cosmetic products on the European Union market, Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) is part of the regulatory framework. Article 8 of Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009 states that cosmetic products must be manufactured in accordance with GMP to ensure the objectives of the Regulation.

Article 8 also states that compliance with GMP is presumed where manufacturing follows relevant harmonised standards whose references have been published in the Official Journal of the European Union. The European Commission's cosmetics harmonised-standards summary lists EN ISO 22716:2007, Cosmetics — Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) — Guidelines on Good Manufacturing Practices. The Commission notes that its summary list is informational and that only publication in the Official Journal gives a harmonised-standard reference legal effect.

This distinction matters to equipment buyers. The Regulation establishes the legal obligation; the harmonised standard provides a recognised framework for GMP practices. Neither should be interpreted as a rule that a particular mixer, tank material, filling principle or automation brand is mandatory for every cosmetic factory.

2. The Role of EN ISO 22716

ISO describes ISO 22716:2007 as guidance covering the production, control, storage and shipment of cosmetic products. ISO states that this edition was reviewed and confirmed in 2022 and remains current. The standard addresses product-quality aspects; its scope does not replace occupational-safety or environmental-protection requirements.

For an equipment project, ISO 22716 is useful because it encourages manufacturers to examine the complete flow of materials and finished products. The mixer is only one part of that flow. Raw-material handling, weighing, processing, intermediate storage, transfer, filling, packaging, cleaning and maintenance all affect the ability to operate consistently.

Important: This article is an equipment-planning guide, not a substitute for the full standard, a site-specific GMP assessment or advice from a qualified regulatory professional. Manufacturers should review the current legal text and the applicable harmonised-standard references for their own operation.

3. Five Equipment Priorities for GMP-Oriented Manufacturing

Priority 1: Product-contact materials and compatibility

Start with the actual formula, not a generic stainless-steel specification. Creams, lotions, gels, cleansing products, alcohol-based liquids and products containing salts or active ingredients can have different compatibility requirements. The selected contact materials, seals, hoses, valves and surface finishes should be reviewed against the product and the cleaning chemicals used by the factory.

SUS304 or SUS316L may be considered for different applications, but neither grade should be treated as an automatic compliance guarantee. Ask the supplier to identify product-contact parts and provide material information relevant to the project. Where corrosion, staining, product interaction or cleaning-agent compatibility is uncertain, arrange a technical review before finalising the specification.

Priority 2: Cleanability and access for inspection

Equipment should be practical to clean using the factory's approved cleaning procedure. Review vessel access, removable components, gasket locations, valve design, pipe routing, drainability and areas where product may remain after discharge. A design that is difficult to inspect or clean can increase changeover time and make routine operation harder to control.

For frequent product changes, consider whether a lifting-cover mixer, removable transfer components, accessible filling parts or a defined cleaning connection would suit the process. CIP capability should be specified only when the equipment design, cleaning method, utilities and process have been assessed together; a CIP connection alone does not prove cleaning effectiveness.

Priority 3: Repeatable process control

Cosmetic product quality can depend on mixing sequence, temperature, agitation speed, homogenising time, vacuum level and cooling profile. Equipment should provide the level of control required by the formula and the manufacturer's process instructions.

For emulsified creams and lotions, a vacuum emulsifying mixer may combine phase preparation, heating, high-shear homogenising, deaeration and controlled cooling. The required configuration depends on viscosity, batch volume, heat-transfer needs, shear sensitivity and discharge method. Recordable process parameters and repeatable operating procedures help the manufacturer compare batches and investigate deviations.

Priority 4: Controlled transfer, holding and filling

After processing, bulk product may need to move through a transfer pump and into a sealed holding or buffer tank before filling. Review whether the transfer method is suitable for the product's viscosity and temperature, whether the tank can be cleaned effectively, and whether the packaging section can receive product at the required rate.

Filling equipment should be selected according to product behaviour, filling volume, container geometry, closure type and target output. Check nozzle positioning, drip control, dosing repeatability, change parts, container handling and the ability to inspect filled units. A stable upstream process can still lose efficiency if the filling and capping section is poorly matched to it.

Priority 5: Documentation, maintenance and operator use

Before purchase, agree what technical documents and support information will be supplied. Depending on the project, useful items may include equipment drawings, product-contact material lists, electrical documentation, operating and maintenance manuals, recommended spare parts, cleaning guidance, instrument lists and test records.

Discuss how temperature sensors, load cells, pressure gauges, vacuum instruments and filling controls will be checked and maintained. A well-designed machine is only one part of a controlled system; operators need clear procedures, training and maintenance support to keep it performing as intended.

5. Match the Equipment to the Product Family

Not every cosmetic product follows the same manufacturing route. An emulsified face cream may require phase preparation, vacuum emulsification and controlled cooling. A color cosmetic powder may require blending, sieving, pan filling and pressing. Lipstick and balm production can involve heated mixing, molding, cooling and dedicated packaging operations.

For cream and lotion projects, review the HONEMIX Cosmetic Cream Production Line and Emulsifying Mixer range. For pressed powder and color cosmetics, see the Color Cosmetic Powder Manufacturing Equipment Solution.

Where the project includes several product families, map each process route separately before deciding which equipment can be shared. Sharing equipment can reduce capital expenditure, but only when product compatibility, cleaning, scheduling and capacity make it practical.

6. Equipment Procurement Checklist

  • List the products, formulas, viscosity ranges and batch sizes to be manufactured.
  • Identify all product-contact parts and review material compatibility.
  • Define cleaning methods, changeover frequency and inspection access.
  • Specify the process parameters that need to be controlled or recorded.
  • Calculate the required output across processing, holding, filling and packaging.
  • Confirm utilities, factory layout, drainage, access and maintenance space.
  • Agree on documentation, operator training, spare parts and service scope.
  • Use representative product and packaging samples for testing before acceptance where practical.

For larger projects, factory acceptance testing (FAT) can be used to review agreed machine functions and project-specific test criteria before shipment. The FAT scope should be defined in advance; it should not be presented as a substitute for the manufacturer's overall GMP responsibilities or site qualification activities.

7. Frequently Asked Questions

Does EU GMP require every cosmetic factory to use a vacuum emulsifying mixer?

No. EU Cosmetics Regulation Article 8 requires cosmetic manufacturing to comply with GMP, but it does not prescribe one mixer type for every product. Equipment should be selected according to the formulation and manufacturing process.

Does buying SUS316L equipment automatically make a factory GMP-compliant?

No. Material selection is one engineering consideration. GMP also involves procedures, people, premises, process control, cleaning, documentation, storage, quality controls and other site-specific practices.

Is ISO 22716 relevant to equipment selection?

Yes. ISO 22716 covers production, control, storage and shipment of cosmetic products. It provides a useful framework for reviewing how equipment supports consistent and controlled manufacturing.

What should a buyer send when requesting a GMP-oriented equipment proposal?

Provide the product type, formula characteristics, batch size, process temperatures, viscosity, cleaning method, target output, container details, factory utilities and any quality-system requirements that affect the design.
