How the EU PPWR Is Changing Cosmetic Packaging and Filling Line Decisions

Understand the PPWR milestones that matter to cosmetic packaging teams and the practical checks to make before changing filling and packaging equipment.

Published November 17, 2026 · HONEMIX Engineering Team
Executive perspective: Regulation (EU) 2025/40, the Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR), has applied since 12 August 2026. For cosmetic manufacturers, the practical response is to review packaging specifications early and test how future container, material, labeling and refill choices affect filling, closing, labeling and secondary-packaging operations.

1. What the PPWR Covers

The Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation, Regulation (EU) 2025/40, establishes EU-wide requirements for packaging and packaging waste. The European Commission explains that the framework covers packaging regardless of material or origin and addresses manufacturing, composition, reusability, recoverability and waste prevention.

The Regulation entered into force in February 2025 and applies from 12 August 2026, with individual obligations phased in on different dates. This means manufacturers should not treat every PPWR requirement as having the same start date. Some obligations apply now, while others depend on future implementing or delegated acts or have 2028, 2030, 2035 or 2038 milestones.

Cosmetic packaging is directly relevant to this framework. The PPWR definition of contact-sensitive packaging includes packaging intended for products covered by Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009 on cosmetic products. The precise requirements still depend on the packaging material, format, role of the economic operator and any applicable exclusions or derogations.

2. Key PPWR Requirements and Dates for Cosmetic Packaging

2.1 Recyclability and design for recycling

Article 6 establishes the principle that packaging placed on the EU market must be recyclable. The detailed design-for-recycling criteria and recyclability performance grades are being developed through EU acts. Under the Regulation, the design-for-recycling assessment and A, B or C grading framework is scheduled to apply from 1 January 2030 or 24 months after the relevant delegated acts enter into force, whichever is later. The recycled-at-scale requirement has a later application stage, linked to 2035 and the relevant implementing acts. From 2038, the Regulation sets a higher threshold by restricting market placement to grades A or B, subject to the detailed rules and exceptions.

For a cosmetic brand, this makes the whole packaging unit important: bottle or jar body, cap, pump, label, sleeve, liner and other components may affect sorting and recycling. A packaging change should therefore be reviewed as a complete system rather than as a container-only decision.

2.2 Recycled content in plastic packaging

Article 7 sets minimum post-consumer recycled-content targets for plastic packaging, calculated as an average per manufacturing plant and year. The target date is 1 January 2030 or three years after the relevant Commission implementing act, whichever is later. The Regulation specifies 30% for contact-sensitive packaging where PET is the major component, 10% for contact-sensitive packaging made from plastics other than PET, and 35% for other plastic packaging, with defined exclusions and derogations.

Because the PPWR definition of contact-sensitive packaging includes packaging for cosmetic products, cosmetic brands should assess the applicable category of each plastic packaging format with their packaging suppliers and compliance advisers. Do not assume that every component or every package is classified in the same way.

Recycled resin can have different mechanical and visual characteristics from virgin resin. Depending on the supplier and package design, a bottle may vary in stiffness, dimensional consistency, surface appearance or behaviour under handling. These are practical engineering risks to test, not automatic properties of every recycled-content package.

2.3 Packaging minimisation

Article 10 requires manufacturers or importers, by 1 January 2030, to ensure that packaging weight and volume are reduced to the minimum necessary to ensure functionality, taking account of packaging shape and material. This does not mean simply choosing the thinnest possible bottle. Product protection, compatibility, dispensing, transport and consumer use still matter.

Article 24 distinguishes between two situations. By 12 February 2028, economic operators filling sales packaging must reduce empty space to the minimum necessary for packaging functionality, including product protection. Separately, operators filling grouped, transport or e-commerce packaging must meet a maximum empty-space ratio of 50% from 1 January 2030 or three years after the relevant implementing acts enter into force, whichever is later. This distinction matters when planning both primary sales packs and secondary shipping configurations.

2.4 Packaging labels and information

Article 12 provides for harmonised packaging labels indicating material composition. The general application date is 12 August 2028 or 24 months after the relevant implementing acts enter into force, whichever is later. The final artwork and label implementation should therefore be coordinated with the applicable technical specifications and national-language requirements rather than designed around an assumed universal label today.

For filling and packaging projects, the operational question is whether label application, coding, inspection and data handling can accommodate the final approved artwork and any additional identification requirements.

2.5 Reuse and refill

Article 28 addresses operators that offer products for purchase through refill. It requires them to communicate rules covering suitable containers, hygiene standards and end-user responsibilities, and to ensure refill stations meet the applicable requirements. This is not a blanket requirement that every cosmetic manufacturer must introduce a refill programme.

Where a brand chooses refill pouches, refill bottles or reusable containers as part of its commercial strategy, the packaging format and filling method should be tested together. Seal integrity, product compatibility, dispensing, cleaning responsibilities and consumer handling all need to be considered.

2.6 Single-use accommodation packaging

Article 25 and Annex V restrict, from 1 January 2030, certain single-use packaging formats. One specifically listed category is single-use accommodation-sector packaging for cosmetics, hygiene and toiletry products intended for an individual booking and discarded before the next guest arrives. The Annex gives examples such as shampoo bottles, hand and body lotion bottles and sachets around bar soap. Hotel and hospitality suppliers should review this category carefully rather than assume that every small cosmetic container is covered.

3. What Packaging Changes Mean for Filling Lines

3.1 Re-test container handling

Changes in bottle or jar material, wall thickness, shape, neck finish or stiffness can affect unscrambling, conveying, gripping, positioning and capping. Before approving a new package, run representative samples through the intended line at realistic operating conditions. Check tipping, scuffing, deformation, jams and container stability.

3.2 Confirm filling and sealing performance

Filling accuracy depends on the product, metering principle, container geometry, nozzle position and operating conditions. A change in package opening, pouch spout, neck finish or material behaviour may require different change parts or adjustments. For refill formats, test the actual pouch, spout, cap or reusable container rather than relying only on a drawing.

3.3 Plan labeling, coding and inspection together

Material-identification labels, revised artwork, QR codes or other data carriers may affect label dimensions, placement, print area and inspection. Confirm the location and readability of required information before finalising the labeler and coding configuration. If an artwork change is expected, allow enough adjustment range in the labeling station and verify the final result with actual containers.

3.4 Keep format changeovers practical

A manufacturer may need to run several bottle sizes, jar formats, refill pouches or closure types. Change parts, guide rails, filling nozzles, cap-handling components and machine recipes should be reviewed for the expected SKU range. The goal is not to make one line accept every package without limits; it is to define a realistic format range and make planned changeovers repeatable.

3.5 Consider the complete packaging route

A packaging redesign can affect primary packaging, labeling, cartoning, shrink wrapping, case packing and palletising. A smaller or differently shaped container may change lane density, carton layout or shipping configuration. The filling machine should therefore be specified as part of a coordinated packaging process.

4. Packaging and Equipment Investment Checklist

  • Identify which PPWR obligations apply to your role as brand owner, packaging manufacturer, importer or other economic operator.
  • Classify each packaging component by material, format and intended use with your packaging supplier or compliance adviser.
  • Obtain final packaging drawings and representative samples before machine approval.
  • Test container handling, filling, sealing, capping, labeling and coding on the proposed line.
  • Review compatibility of the formula with the proposed packaging material, closure and dispensing system.
  • Check whether secondary packaging dimensions and empty-space ratios need to change.
  • Define the SKU range, change parts, changeover method and expected changeover time.
  • Keep supplier specifications and packaging test results with the project documentation.
Equipment boundary: A filling-machine supplier can help test and configure equipment for specified containers and closures. The machine supplier does not determine whether a cosmetic package complies with PPWR. Packaging conformity, technical documentation and the responsibilities of each economic operator must be assessed separately.

5. Plan Equipment Around Confirmed Packaging Specifications

Do not order a filling line based only on a concept image or an early packaging sample. Ask the packaging supplier for final dimensions, material structure, neck or spout details, tolerances, closure specifications and labeling zones. Then test the package with the product and the intended filling and closing process.

6. Frequently Asked Questions

Does the PPWR apply to cosmetic packaging?

Yes. The PPWR applies broadly to packaging placed on the EU market, and its definition of contact-sensitive packaging expressly includes packaging intended for cosmetic products covered by Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009. Specific obligations depend on material, format, role and applicable exemptions.

Does every cosmetic company need to switch to refill packaging?

No. The Regulation addresses operators that offer refill and establishes certain refill-related requirements. It does not impose a universal requirement for every cosmetic brand to launch refill packaging.

Will recycled plastic require a new filling machine?

Not necessarily. The correct response is to test the selected packaging on the existing or proposed line. Some formats may run with existing equipment after adjustment; others may need new change parts or handling modifications.

Should manufacturers wait until 2030 before reviewing packaging?

No. Packaging design, supplier selection, artwork, tooling and machine validation take time. Reviewing material and format options early gives manufacturers more time to test changes before relevant milestones.
